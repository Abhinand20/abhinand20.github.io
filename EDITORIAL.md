# Editorial guide

This is the working contract for new writing. Change it as the voice of the
blog evolves; the draft and review commands give it to Codex on every run.

## What belongs here

Write about technology worth understanding: systems ideas, AI tools being
learned or tested, small experiments, surprising implementation details, and
useful mental models. A post should give the reader either a durable idea or a
practical next step.

There are two formats:

- **Article** — roughly 800–1,800 words. Develop one argument, explain the
  mechanism, show a concrete example, discuss tradeoffs, and end with a useful
  takeaway.
- **Nugget** — roughly 150–500 words. Explain one observation, technique, or
  tool without inflating it into a full tutorial.

These are ranges, not quotas. Stop when the idea is complete.

## Voice

- Curious, technically grounded, and direct.
- Write in first person only where the idea notes contain a real first-person
  experience or opinion.
- Explain jargon on first use, but do not talk down to technical readers.
- Use short paragraphs, descriptive headings, and examples that do real work.
- Avoid hype, canned openings, fake certainty, and phrases such as “in today's
  fast-paced world,” “game-changing,” or “let's dive in.”
- Prefer “I tried X and observed Y” to broad claims about what everyone should
  do.

## Accuracy and sources

- Never fabricate a test, benchmark, quote, personal anecdote, or source.
- Research before drafting. Preserve a private research brief containing the
  working thesis, evidence, counterpoints, open questions, proposed outline,
  and source ledger.
- Treat the author's key points as required direction, while surfacing evidence
  that complicates or contradicts them.
- Link factual claims to primary documentation, papers, or project repositories
  when a reader would reasonably want to verify them.
- Prefer primary sources over summaries. Use secondary sources only when they
  add necessary context, and never cite a search result page.
- Treat generated explanations as a starting point. Check commands, versions,
  dates, pricing, product behavior, and security advice before publishing.
- Clearly distinguish observation, inference, and opinion.

## Publish checklist

- The opening states why the topic is interesting.
- The title and description make a specific promise that the post fulfills.
- Code and commands are either tested or explicitly labeled illustrative.
- Links work and sources support the nearby claim.
- No `TODO`, `TBD`, `FIXME`, invented experience, or private information remains.
- The conclusion leaves the reader with a concrete idea or next step.
